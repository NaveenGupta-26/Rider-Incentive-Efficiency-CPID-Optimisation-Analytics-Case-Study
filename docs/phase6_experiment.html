06A/B Experiment Design
The Full Experiment Spec:
Three Arms, Five Weeks, One Clear Winner
Phase 6 · 3-arm controlled experiment · Cluster randomisation · Rider-first guard rails

The SQL and statistical analysis told us which incentive type performs best in our historical data. But historical data has a limitation: riders in different incentive groups weren't chosen randomly — the platform already assigned them based on policies. Before recommending a full budget shift, we need a controlled experiment where assignment is random, conditions are isolated, and we can make a genuinely causal claim about which bonus structure works better.

Here is the complete experiment design — written the way you'd present it to a Head of Growth before getting approval to run it.

Why Variant B uses a hybrid model, not pure surge

When we simulated pure surge top-up (₹17/hr all hours), rider earnings dropped ₹344/week compared to the control — the rider-first guard rail failed. The reason: surge top-up pays per online hour, but our riders average 51 hours online and only 104 deliveries/week. At ₹17/hr that's ₹867 in incentives vs ₹1,248 under the flat model. The hybrid fix — ₹8/delivery reduced flat + ₹35/peak-hour surge — gives riders ₹6,262/week: ₹361 more than the control while still improving CPID. Both platform and riders win.

Why 15% MDE and 5 weeks is the right call

10% MDE requires 10 weeks — long enough that seasonal changes (festival season, weather) start contaminating the signal. 20% MDE needs only 3 weeks but means we'd only detect very large effects, missing modest but real improvements that are worth acting on. 5 weeks at 15% MDE balances detection sensitivity with experimental feasibility. The observed effect sizes in our data are 12–24%, so a 15% MDE threshold means both variants have a real chance of being declared a winner.

Why the high standard deviation is honest, not a problem

The std dev of ₹549.9 looks alarming for a metric with a median of ₹60.8. This is the right-skew problem: some rider-weeks show CPID in the thousands because the rider happened to generate almost zero incremental deliveries in an incentive week (illness, personal reasons). These are real observations, not errors. The high std dev means we need more observations to detect a given effect size — which is why 5 weeks (not 2–3) is the honest recommendation. An analyst who fakes a low std dev to justify a shorter experiment is setting up a false positive.

SUTVANetwork Effects + Cluster Randomisation
The Problem With Rider-Level
Randomisation
SUTVA violation · Spillover · Cluster randomisation at city level

The most technically sophisticated part of this experiment design is also the part most portfolios get wrong. The temptation is to randomise at the individual rider level — assign each rider randomly to Control, Variant A, or Variant B. Simple, clean, maximum statistical power. But it has a fundamental flaw.

What SUTVA is and why it matters here

SUTVA stands for the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption. It states that one person's treatment should not affect another person's outcome. In a drug trial, giving Patient A the treatment doesn't change whether Patient B responds to theirs. SUTVA is satisfied.

In our experiment, SUTVA is violated if we randomise at the rider level. Here's why: Rider A in Mumbai is assigned to Variant B (hybrid surge) and comes online 2 hours earlier because the peak-hour bonus is attractive. Rider B in Mumbai is assigned to Control (flat per-order). Because Rider A is online and accepting orders during those extra 2 hours, there are fewer orders left for Rider B. Rider B's measured "incremental deliveries" drops — not because of anything about flat bonuses, but because Rider A took orders that would have gone to Rider B.

This spillover effect contaminates both the control and treatment measurements. The experiment would understate the true benefit of Variant B and potentially create spurious negative effects on the control arm.

The fix — cluster randomisation at city level
Randomisation unitCity — each entire city is assigned to one arm. All riders in Mumbai get the same incentive structure. All riders in Pune get a different one.
City assignments Control: Mumbai, Pune, Surat
Variant A: Delhi, Jaipur, Bhopal
Variant B: Bengaluru, Ahmedabad, Coimbatore
Why these citiesEach arm gets one Tier-1, one Tier-2, one Tier-3 city — balanced by city tier so the comparison isn't confounded by supply saturation differences
Riders per armControl: ~599 (Mumbai 238 + Pune 176 + Surat 123) · Variant A: ~554 · Variant B: ~626 — roughly balanced
The trade-off of cluster randomisation

Cluster randomisation eliminates spillover but reduces statistical power because cities are not perfectly interchangeable — Mumbai has 238 riders and a different order density than Bhopal with 126 riders. The variance between cities adds noise. To compensate, we use a paired analysis: compare cities in each arm to matched baseline cities from the pre-experiment period, rather than just comparing arm averages. This is the Difference-in-Differences approach applied at city level.

Why bringing up SUTVA in an interview immediately separates you

Most A/B test descriptions in portfolios say "randomise riders to three arms and compare." Asking "but wait — do riders in different arms compete for the same orders?" is a question that signals you understand that experiments in marketplace and platform contexts are fundamentally different from simple product A/B tests. Every delivery platform, ride-sharing company, and marketplace has this problem. Knowing the name (SUTVA violation), the mechanism (spillover), and the fix (cluster randomisation) is a senior-level signal.

Guard RailsWhat Stops the Experiment Early
The Lines We
Do Not Cross
Three guard rail metrics · Early stopping rules · Rider-first commitment

Guard rails are metrics that, if breached, stop the experiment regardless of what the primary metric shows. A variant can have perfect CPID and still fail if it harms riders, collapses supply, or creates a satisfaction crisis that takes months to recover from. Here are the three guard rails and the exact thresholds that trigger a stop.

Guard rail 1 — rider weekly earnings
What we measureAverage weekly take-home earnings per rider (base pay + incentive) in each arm
ThresholdMust not fall below control arm by more than 3% (₹5,901 × 0.97 = ₹5,724 floor)
Current Var B result₹6,262/week — ₹361 above control ✓ The hybrid surge model was specifically designed to exceed this threshold
Why 3%?A 3% earnings drop on a ₹5,901 base = ₹177/week. At the documented rider expense structure (₹200–₹350/day fuel), even a ₹177 weekly cut can push a rider's net into negative territory. Zero tolerance on earnings cuts is the only defensible position given the AIF finding that 68% of riders already say expenses exceed earnings.
Guard rail 2 — delivery coverage rate
What we measure% of platform orders that receive a rider assignment within 10 minutes
ThresholdMust not fall below 90% of the pre-experiment coverage rate in any experimental city
Why this mattersAn incentive structure that improves CPID by reducing supply (fewer riders online) is not a win — it's just moving the cost from the incentive budget to unfulfilled orders and customer churn
Guard rail 3 — rider churn rate
What we measure% of active riders in each arm who go inactive (zero orders) for 2+ consecutive weeks during the experiment
ThresholdMust not exceed 1.5× the control arm churn rate
Why this mattersChurn is the delayed signal. A rider might not quit in week 1 of a new incentive structure — but if the structure is creating stress or income insecurity, you'll see exits in weeks 3–5. Monitoring it throughout (not just at end) is essential.
Weekly check-in protocol
Week 1Confirm arm assignments. Baseline guard rail metrics recorded. No stopping decisions yet.
Weeks 2–4Weekly guard rail review. Any arm breaching earnings or coverage threshold: pause that arm, revert to control, investigate before restarting.
Week 5 (final)Primary metric analysis. Statistical test. Winner declaration only if all guard rails are green across all 5 weeks.
Why Variant B was redesigned before this tab was written

When we simulated the pure surge top-up (₹17/hr), rider earnings dropped ₹344/week — failing the earnings guard rail immediately. Rather than ignoring this finding or burying it, we redesigned Variant B as a hybrid model (₹8/delivery + ₹35/peak hour) that passes the guard rail with ₹361 headroom above the control. That redesign happened because the guard rail forced us to think about rider welfare before finalising the experiment design. This is the right order of operations — and it's worth explaining explicitly when presenting this project.

Rollout3-Phase Deployment Plan
From Experiment Winner
to Platform-Wide Policy
3-phase rollout · Starting with Tier-2/3 · Full deployment in 12 weeks

Declaring a winner is not the finish line. Rolling out a new incentive structure to 350,000+ riders across 500+ cities without creating a supply crisis requires a staged approach. Here is the 3-phase plan.

Phase 1 — Weeks 1–3 post-experiment
Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities first
Roll out hybrid surge to all Tier-2 (Pune, Jaipur, Lucknow, Ahmedabad) and Tier-3 cities. These cities have the best CPID efficiency and the most room for supply response. Risk is lower here — if the rollout has unexpected issues, the affected rider base is smaller and the GMV impact is manageable. Run for 3 weeks and confirm CPID and earnings match experiment results before proceeding.
Phase 2 — Weeks 4–8
Tier-1 cities with modified parameters
Mumbai, Delhi, Bengaluru require a different calibration. Tier-1 riders are at higher daily delivery volumes — the peak-hour surge timing window needs to be optimised for the specific demand patterns in each metro. Run a mini-experiment within each Tier-1 city to calibrate the peak-hour rate (₹35/hr may need adjustment for Mumbai's 16-hour workday density). Confirm earnings guard rail holds before full deployment.
Phase 3 — Weeks 9–12
Full platform deployment + monitoring dashboard live
Platform-wide rollout. The Phase 5 dashboard (Tableau/Looker) goes live with all guard rail KPI tiles active. Weekly review for the first 4 weeks post-full-deployment. Specifically watch: cliff effect in weeks immediately after any city transitions from old to new structure, and any rider satisfaction survey signals from the Tier-1 cities where the workday is most intense.
What to do with always-on riders during rollout

Veterans (12+ months, identified as always-on from Phase 3 Q3) are a special case. The hybrid surge model is less efficient for them because they're already online maximum hours — the peak-hour bonus doesn't pull them online longer. For this segment, the recommendation is a tenure-based bonus instead: a monthly completion bonus (e.g. ₹3,000 for 2,000+ deliveries/month) that rewards sustained high performance without trying to change behaviour that doesn't need changing. This keeps their earnings stable while redirecting the per-delivery flat spend toward more elastic riders.

The single most important thing to monitor post-rollout

Rider satisfaction survey scores in weeks 2–4 post-rollout. Earnings improving is necessary but not sufficient — riders also care about predictability. The old flat bonus was simple and predictable: ₹12/delivery, every delivery, always. The hybrid surge model is more complex: some hours pay more, some less. If rider confusion about their pay structure increases, satisfaction scores will drop even if total earnings are higher. The rollout communications must explain the new structure clearly, in the local language, before the change goes live. This is not an analytics problem — it's a change management problem. Flag it before it becomes a rider relations crisis.
